## Fields

**Title:**
The Unphrasable Law: What Happens When the Principle of Lawfulness Is Turned on Its Formulators

**Author:** David Hoze

**Publication status:** Unpublished manuscript (2026). If the form offers a link field, you can also add the Substack URL as the online location.

**Abstract:**
If every phenomenon falls under law, there should be a law of our own thinking: a formal system whose theorems are exactly what we can justifiably establish. This essay argues that if such a law exists, we can never hold it - never simultaneously possess its formulation, know that it is ours, and know that it is sound. The reason is a structural flexibility: whoever holds and trusts a rulebook can establish truths the rulebook cannot (its own consistency, by Godel's second incompleteness theorem), and therefore exceeds any book he can hold. The argument is developed in a lay idiom - rulebooks, certificates, floors - with formal backing in appendices (Godel, Lob, Tarski, Goodstein, Turing progressions, Reinhardt-Carlson epistemic arithmetic). Against Lucas and Penrose, nothing anti-mechanist follows: mechanism is possible but unknowable in the first person. The argument extends to humanity as a collective knower, which, unlike the individual, has no "outside." Finally, the only entity that could truthfully declare its own reliability is one whose saying constitutes reality - so the intuition of total lawfulness was always, in disguise, a theological one.

**Keywords:**
incompleteness, Lob's theorem, Tarski's theorem, mechanism, self-knowledge, Lucas-Penrose argument, epistemic arithmetic, Goodstein's theorem, faith
