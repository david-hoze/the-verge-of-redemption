# Apophatic Tolerance

Tolerance has a justification problem. Not a practical problem — practically, we all know why we need it — but a philosophical one. The two standard defenses both fail, and they fail in instructive ways.

The first defense is skeptical: nobody really knows the truth, so nobody has standing to impose their view. This buys tolerance at the price of conviction. If my reason for tolerating you is that I might be wrong about everything, then I have also lost my reason for believing anything. Skeptical tolerance is corrosive; it doesn't teach me to respect your view, it teaches me to stop taking views seriously — mine included. And it is self-undermining in the familiar way: the claim "nobody knows the truth" is itself a truth-claim asserted with some confidence.

The second defense is relativist: all views are equally true, each "true for" its holder. This is worse. If your view and mine genuinely contradict each other — you say the act is forbidden, I say it is obligatory — then declaring both true is not generosity, it is incoherence. From a contradiction, everything follows. A tolerance built on "everyone is right" collapses into a position from which nothing can be affirmed or denied, which is to say, no position at all.

So the choice appears to be: tolerance without conviction, or conviction without tolerance. I want to argue there is a third structure available, and that it depends on a distinction that I have found useful in other contexts — between two modes of ineffability that are habitually confused with each other.

## Gap and glut

Call them **פער** and **עודף** — gap and glut.

A _glut_ is a violation of the law of non-contradiction: P and not-P, both asserted, both meant. Gluts are explosive. A framework that tolerates a single genuine glut can prove anything and therefore says nothing. When mystics or theologians are accused of talking nonsense, the accusation is usually that they are trafficking in gluts.

A _gap_ is something else entirely. A gap is a failure of the law of excluded middle at the level of our concepts: a region of reality where neither P nor not-P is assertable — not because there is no fact of the matter, but because the predicates we would need to state the fact do not reach that far. "Is the number seven happy?" is not answered by "yes" or "no"; the question suffers presupposition-failure. The apophatic tradition, read carefully, is a tradition of gaps, not gluts. When Maimonides says that God's knowledge and God's self are one, he is not asserting a contradiction; he is flagging that our subject–attribute grammar fails before it arrives. The statement is not false and not meaningless — it marks the precise point where a conceptual scheme runs out.

The distinction matters because gaps are _coherent_ and gluts are not. A gap says: there is more here than this language can phrase. A glut says: this language phrases it, and it is contradictory. The first is an honest report on the limits of a net; the second tears the net.

Now apply this to disagreement.

## Why the universe supports more than one true description

Here is the metaphysical picture I want to defend, stated plainly: reality is richer than any single conceptual scheme. Not infinitely plastic — there are wrong descriptions, plenty of them — but _over-determined_ relative to any one net we throw over it. Every serious framework is a projection: it flattens a higher-dimensional object onto the plane its concepts define, and different frameworks flatten along different axes. Each projection captures real structure. None captures all of it. And — this is the crucial point — the projections can _appear_ to contradict each other, in exactly the way a cylinder's shadow is a circle from one angle and a rectangle from another.

If you only have the shadows, "it is a circle" and "it is a rectangle" look like a glut. They are not. They are two true partial descriptions whose reconciliation lives one dimension up — in a space you may not be able to enter. The contradiction is an artifact of the flattening.

This is the structure of what I am calling _apophatic tolerance_. I tolerate your view, not because I doubt mine (skepticism), and not because I grant yours equal truth as phrased (relativism, glut), but because I hold that the reality we are both describing exceeds both of our descriptions, and that the standpoint from which our descriptions would be reconciled exists — while remaining, for creatures with our conceptual equipment, unoccupiable. The unity of our views is real and unsayable. That is the apophatic move: not "there is no answer," and not "both answers are true," but "the answer is located past the point where our predicates fail."

Notice what this preserves. It preserves my conviction: my projection really does capture what it captures, and I am entitled — obligated, even — to defend it against descriptions that get the shadow wrong. It preserves the seriousness of disagreement: we are not "each right in our own way"; we are each partially right about the same one thing, and it matters which parts. And it preserves humility without skepticism: what I lack is not justification for my view but access to the vantage from which my view and yours are one.

## The Breslov deepening

I did not invent this structure, and I want to acknowledge the tradition where I found its sharpest formulation — because that tradition adds something the philosophical skeleton lacks.

Rebbe Nachman of Breslov teaches (Likutei Moharan 64) that there is a class of questions — _kushiyot_ — that arise from the חלל הפנוי, the "vacated space" of the Lurianic creation-story: the region from which the infinite light was withdrawn so that a finite world could stand. Questions arising from that space cannot be answered — not because they have no answers, but because the intellect in which their answers live is the very intellect that was withdrawn to make room for us. The answers exist; they are simply not present _in the space we occupy_. This is a gap-claim of astonishing precision: not "the question is meaningless," not "both answers are true," but "the resolution is real and located exactly where our comprehension, by design, does not extend."

And then Rebbe Nachman makes a move I find remarkable. He says (ibid., §4) that disagreement itself — the machloket between scholars — _is_ an instance of the vacated space. If all the sages were one, there would be no room for a world; it is precisely their pulling apart, each drawing toward his own side, that creates between them the חלל הפנוי in which creation happens. Disagreement is not a bug in the fabric; it is the fabric. The empty space between your view and mine is structurally the same as the empty space in which anything exists at all.

R. Natan of Nemirov, Rebbe Nachman's disciple, draws the consequence for the classic rabbinic formula. When the Talmud says of the schools of Hillel and Shammai, _elu va-elu divrei Elokim chayim_ — "these and those are the words of the living God" — the lazy reading is a glut: both contradictory rulings true. R. Natan reads it as a gap: "in truth it is all one — only that this cannot be grasped by human intellect in any manner whatsoever" (Likutei Halakhot, Mezuzah 4:4). The unity of the contradictory positions is affirmed and located above the reach of the faculties doing the disagreeing. Elsewhere (Shomer Sachar 2:10) he generalizes: all the wills of all people, however divergent, flow from a single root-will — _rava d'ravin_, the will behind the wills — "only that it is impossible to understand this." Two sincere people can share the very same underlying desire and fight bitterly, because each sees only the other's projection and cannot see the root from which both projections descend.

What the Hasidic material adds to the philosophical skeleton is an _attitude_ fitted to gaps. If the resolution to a kushiya is real but unreachable, then the rational response is neither to force an answer (which produces gluts — strained harmonizations that assert contradictions with a straight face) nor to conclude there is none (which produces despair or contempt). Rebbe Nachman's language is לדלג ולקפוץ — to leap and skip over the question. The leap is not a refusal to think. It is the recognition, itself an act of intellect, that this particular question is gap-typed: its answer exists on the far side of a conceptual boundary I cannot cross, and therefore withholding judgment while maintaining trust in the resolution is the epistemically correct posture — not a pious evasion of it. The tradition calls this posture _emunah_. I would call it the appropriate propositional attitude toward truths one has principled grounds to believe exist but no capacity to phrase.

There is even a Gödelian resonance here, which readers of my earlier essays will anticipate. No formal system can certify its own consistency from within; the certification, if it exists, lives one system up. Likewise no conceptual framework can adjudicate between itself and a rival from inside itself; the adjudication, if it exists, lives one framework up — in a standpoint that, for us, functions as a limit rather than a location. The vacated space is where our proofs run out. That our proofs run out there is itself something we can prove.

## What apophatic tolerance does not tolerate

A framework of tolerance that tolerates everything is worthless, and this one does not.

The gap/glut distinction cuts both ways. Apophatic tolerance shelters _gap-type_ disagreements: cases where two views, each internally coherent, each answerable to reality, each held in good faith, capture different projections of something that exceeds both. It does not shelter gluts — views that contradict not my projection but the object itself. The person who calls cruelty kindness is not flattening the cylinder along a different axis; he is describing a different object, or no object. There is no higher vantage from which cruelty and kindness are reconciled, because the claim "cruelty is kindness" is not a partial truth awaiting completion — it is a falsehood, complete as it stands.

The Hasidic sources build this boundary in from the start. R. Natan's account of reconciled disagreement applies to those whose intention is _leshem shamayim_ — oriented, however differently, toward the good and the true. The differences that flow from the root-will are differences of soul-root, of measure, of path: one soul is built for lovingkindness and another for rigor, one for study and another for prayer, and from below the paths look opposed while from the root they interlock. But a will oriented _against_ the root is not one more projection of it. Discerning which is which is genuinely hard, and the tradition's answer — that the discernment ultimately requires a vantage higher than our own — is honest about the difficulty rather than a solution to it. Apophatic tolerance therefore comes with a standing warning: the same humility that stops me from damning your projection should stop me from canonizing my judgment that a view is beyond the pale. But the _category_ of beyond-the-pale remains, and must, or the whole structure collapses back into the relativist glut it was built to escape.

## The elephant, re-read

The blind men and the elephant is usually told as a relativist parable, and as such it deserves the contempt it gets: the storyteller, conveniently, sees the whole elephant, which is exactly the standpoint the parable claims nobody has. But re-read it as a gap parable and it comes right. The men are not "each correct in his own way." Each is _strictly_ correct about a real part — the trunk really is snakelike, the leg really is pillarlike — and strictly wrong the moment he asserts his part as the whole. The elephant exists; the reconciliation of their reports exists; and no one in the story occupies the position from which it can be stated. The moral is not "all views are equal." The moral is: hold your rope firmly, report it accurately, defend your description against people who say ropes are pillars — and remember that the animal is larger than your hands.

That is apophatic tolerance. Not the weak tolerance of the skeptic, who has nothing to defend, and not the incoherent tolerance of the relativist, who defends everything and therefore nothing. It is the tolerance of a person who believes there is one truth, believes his own view genuinely touches it, and believes — with reasons, not merely with warmth — that the point at which his view and his opponent's are one lies just past the last predicate either of them owns. What unites us is not nothing. It is unsayable, which is a different thing entirely — and learning to keep those two apart may be most of what tolerance is.
