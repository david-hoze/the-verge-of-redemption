# The System With Many Wheels

## You Are Never Fighting About What You Are Fighting About

*Fences* (2016). Directed by Denzel Washington. Denzel Washington, Viola Davis, Jovan Adepo, Stephen Henderson, Mykelti Williamson. Troy Maxson is building a fence. Every Saturday, he and his friend Bono work on it - measuring, cutting, nailing boards into place. His wife Rose asked for the fence. Troy cannot explain why he keeps putting it off, and he cannot explain why he keeps coming back to it. The fence is the thing Troy can do with his hands when everything his hands cannot fix is falling apart.

Troy was a baseball player - a great one - who came up in the Negro Leagues when the color line still held. By the time Jackie Robinson crossed it, Troy was too old. He became a garbage collector. He is proud of providing. He shows up. He brings the paycheck home on Friday. He does not drink it away. He tells his son Cory that he does not have to like him - "I owe you. I give you your life." The provision is impeccable. The presence is gone.

Rose watches him build the fence and understands something Troy cannot see: the fence is not the marriage. The fence is what happens when the center of the marriage goes dark and the man keeps building at the edges because building is the only language he has. Troy's honor axis - his competence, his reliability, his status as the man who provides - is fully operational. Every peripheral wheel in the system is turning. The central wheel - the one where Rose lives, the one that runs on connection, on being wanted, on the evidence that she exists in his mind as a person and not as a role - has stopped.

When Rose discovers Troy's affair, she does not list his failures. She names the system: "I gave eighteen years of my life to stand in the same spot as you. I took all my feelings, my wants and needs and dreams, and I buried them inside you. I planted myself inside you and waited to bloom. And it didn't take me no eighteen years to find out the soil was hard and rocky and it wasn't never gonna bloom. But I held on to you." She is describing a system in which every visible part was working and the invisible center was dead.

## The System

A marriage is not a relationship. It is a system.

A system is a collection of different parts, each bringing a specific function to the whole. If one part is missing or displaced, it can disable part of the system - or all of it. A systemic marriage counselor I trained under taught this as the foundational principle: when a couple comes in with a problem, the problem they present is almost never the problem. It is a symptom - a peripheral wheel that has stopped turning because something at the center has seized.

The Maharal writes that man and woman are not naturally compatible. They oppose each other like fire and water. And yet they are a system. The opposition is not a defect in the system. It is the system. Fire and water do not blend. They do not become a third substance. They remain opposed - and the structure that holds them in opposition is the marriage.

A system composed of identical parts is not a system. It is a stockpile. The fact that he operates on the honor axis and she operates on the connection axis is not a problem to be resolved. It is the reason the system has more than one wheel.

## Two Central Wheels, Many Peripheral Ones

Every marriage system has two central axes. His runs on honor - respect, trust, the sense that he is relied upon and that his place in the structure is real. Hers runs on connection - love, affection, the sense that she is wanted and that the space between them is warm.

From these central axes, peripheral wheels develop. The household logistics. The division of labor. The children's routines. The extended family. The social life. The finances. The intimacy. Each of these is a wheel in the system, and each one is connected - through gears that are not always visible - to one of the two central axes.

When you want to restart a stalled system, you have a choice. You can try to move the central wheel directly - address the honor axis or the connection axis head-on. This is the most impactful intervention, but also the hardest, because the central axes are inflamed, defensive, raw. Or you can find a small peripheral wheel that clearly connects to the center and move that instead. Less resistance. Less pain. And if the connection to the center is real, the small movement transmits.

The counseling tradition teaches: find something easy, but with a visible connection to the central axis. Five minutes a day. Sit together. Something warm to drink. Tell each other one thing that happened today. Add one word of appreciation. This is not the central wheel. But it touches it. The five minutes are a peripheral wheel that connects to the center through the simplest gear: evidence of attention.

## The Socks on the Floor

A woman comes to the counselor's office furious about socks. Her husband leaves his socks on the floor every day. She has asked a hundred times. He does not change. She is at the end of her capacity.

The socks are not the problem.

When the counselor enters the system - when she follows the gear train from the peripheral wheel to the center - the socks lead to disorder, disorder leads to "he's not responsible," irresponsibility leads to "he's not a real partner in this house," and "not a partner" leads to the central axis: I am alone. I am running this house by myself. He lives here like a guest whose meals are prepared by someone else. The socks on the floor are the outermost wheel. The aloneness is the center.

He does not understand why she is so upset about socks. Because on his axis, socks are socks. A logistical issue. He will try to remember. But for her, the socks are not a logistical issue. They are a daily reminder that the central wheel of partnership has stopped turning, and no one in the house has noticed except her.

This is the systemic principle that changes everything: in a marriage, every small issue is connected to the center, and the center is always one of two things - his honor or her connection. The fight about dishes is a fight about partnership. The fight about his mother is a fight about being chosen. The fight about money is a fight about trust. The fight about how he said that thing at the dinner party is a fight about respect. You are never fighting about what you are fighting about.

The counselor who treats the socks - who offers organizational tips, who suggests a hamper by the bed - is treating a peripheral wheel while the central axis rusts. The counselor who sees the system follows the gears inward and finds the woman who is alone in her own house.

## The Child Who Got Sick

The system extends beyond the couple.

There is a concept in family therapy called the identified patient. A child develops symptoms - anxiety, compulsive behaviors, psychosomatic illness - and the family brings the child for treatment. The child is the patient. The child is not the problem.

A marriage counselor in this tradition told the story of a child who was chronically ill. The doctors could not find a cause. The body was simply weak - one infection after another, no recovery, no explanation. They sent the family to a child psychologist who also worked with family systems.

The psychologist discovered that when the child was very young, the parents had been in severe marital distress. But when the child got sick, the parents rallied. They came together. They cooperated. They stopped fighting. The child's body learned - not consciously, not deliberately - that illness was the price of his parents' peace. The psychosomatic response was real. The illness was real. The cause was the system.

When the parents' marriage improved, the child stopped getting sick.

The identified patient is the system's way of speaking when the people inside it cannot. The child who acts out, the teenager who withdraws, the toddler who regresses - these are often peripheral wheels that have absorbed the damage from a central axis that stopped turning. The family brings the child. The system brings the marriage.

## What the Fence Was For

Troy Maxson builds the fence because he cannot build the thing that needs building. The fence has posts and boards and nails. It is measurable. It makes progress. You can see it going up. The marriage has no posts. It makes no visible progress. He cannot point to it and say: I built that today.

Every wheel in Troy's system is turning except the one that matters. He provides. He shows up. He disciplines his son. He tells stories on Friday nights. The peripheral wheels are immaculate. The center - the place where Rose lives, where she planted herself and waited to bloom - is hard and rocky and has been hard and rocky since before the fence began.

Rose sees the system. She has always seen it. She did not need a counselor to map the gears. She knows that the provision is not the problem and the fence is not the solution. She knows that every board Troy nails into place is a board he is not placing at the center. But she also knows - and this is the part that breaks the heart - that Troy does not have the lumber for the center. His father broke it out of him before he was old enough to know what it was. The honor axis was damaged at the root. What he offers - the work, the paycheck, the fence - is genuinely the best he can access.

The system is not fooled. The peripheral wheels can turn for years. The center knows.

## Starting the System

When a system has stalled, the instinct is to go straight to the center. Fix the big thing. Address the real issue. Say the hard truth.

The tradition teaches the opposite: start small. Start at a peripheral wheel that the couple can actually move. Give them a task for one week - not a lifestyle change, not a commitment forever. One week. Something specific, something measurable, something that connects to the center through a visible gear.

She will sit with him for five minutes when he comes home. Not to discuss the budget. Not to review the children's schedules. To ask: how was your day? To receive the answer. To offer one word of appreciation. This is a peripheral wheel. It connects to the center because five minutes of undivided attention is the smallest possible evidence that she exists in his mind.

He will express willingness to help with one household task - not because it is needed, but because the willingness is the message. The task is the peripheral wheel. The message - I am a partner in this house, not a guest - is the gear that connects it to her central axis.

One week. If it works, they will want to continue. If it doesn't, the counselor can investigate why. But the principle holds: small movements at the edge can restart the center, because in a system, every wheel is connected to every other wheel, and the gears do not care which direction the force comes from.

The fence was never going to save the marriage. But five minutes on the porch - with nothing to build and nothing to prove - might have.
