---"Most of the mistakes I've made have come from urgency." That line is worth the whole piece. It's honest and it's true - and it's the kind of thing most people only realize after the damage is done.

There's something in this that reminds me of Shabbat - but Shabbat is more extreme than what you're describing. It doesn't rebrand slow. It commands stopping. One day in seven. Not a practice you adopt when you feel ready - a structural interruption that arrives whether you've finished or not.

No producing, no fixing, no optimizing. The candles are lit, the work stops, and for twenty-five hours you're a person who isn't getting anything done. What I've found is that the compulsory part is what makes it work - because the people who most need to stop are the ones who won't do it voluntarily. I include myself in that.

The tradition didn't trust people to find the balance on their own. It built the balance into the calendar. That's the part that changed things for me - not the insight that slowness matters, but having a structure that enforces it before I'm ready. I wrote about why the structure matters more than the insight here: [https://davidhoze.substack.com/p/the-muscle-you-stopped-using](https://davidhoze.substack.com/p/the-muscle-you-stopped-using)
