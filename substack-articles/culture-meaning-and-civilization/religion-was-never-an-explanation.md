# Religion Was Never an Explanation

*Fiddler on the Roof (1971). Directed by Norman Jewison. Starring Topol, Norma Crane, Leonard Frey. Tevye the dairyman argues with God all day long, out loud, on a first-name basis, the way you argue with a partner you cannot divorce. He never once tries to prove God exists. That was settled long before the film began. What is left is the conversation inside the covenant, and the tradition that holds the terms.*

For a few hundred years now, science has been winning a fight that never happened.

The story everyone learned goes like this. Once, religion explained the world. Lightning was God's anger, plague was God's judgment, the sun crossed the sky because that was the route God set for it. Then science arrived with better explanations and took the territory back, one field at a time, until religion was left holding only the gaps, the shrinking set of things we cannot yet account for. God of the gaps. And the gaps keep closing.

It is a clean story. It is also a category error in the first sentence.

## Religion doesn't explain. It asserts.

Done honestly, a religion is not in the business of physical explanation at all. Take the one I know. The Jewish claim is not "here is a mechanism for lightning." It believes in divine providence, so it says that behind the lightning there is a divine will. And behind the making of the world too. It is not occupied with the "how," it is occupied with the "why." Not with how the hand of providence moves through the world, but with what the one moving the hand wants.

And the reason to believe is not that God made the world. The reason is a covenant. A contract. We were slaves in Egypt. Moses prophesied as God's messenger, and everything that came out of his mouth came to pass, one for one. After ten plagues, after which the sea split, he took us out of Egypt. We received a Torah at Sinai, and the message was plain: I saved you, you are now my people, here are the terms. That is not a hypothesis competing with thermodynamics. It is testimony, and a deal struck on the strength of it.

Notice what I am not doing. I am not fighting evolution, and I am not fighting the big bang. Maybe they happened, maybe they did not; I have no stake in the mechanism either way. What certainly happened is that we came out of Egypt. You can hand me the most complete physics in the world, finished to the last decimal, and it does not move that by an inch, because it was never an answer to the same question.

You cannot beat a religious assertion with a better explanation, because they are not the same kind of move. It is like answering a marriage with a weather report. When a physicist explains how the eye evolved, he has not refuted a covenant any more than a better map refutes a promise. The two were never reaching for the same thing. One says how the machinery runs. The other says who you belong to and what you owe. Science can be completely correct and leave the contract entirely untouched, because the contract never made a claim about the machinery.

So when people say "science disproved religion," listen to what is actually being said: a tool that measures mechanism found no mechanism where the believer was not pointing at mechanism in the first place.

## But what about what's written in Genesis?

Here comes the obvious objection. Fine, maybe religion does not explain lightning. But Genesis does explain. Six days of creation, the order of things, where it all came from. Is that not a mechanism competing with science's story of the origin?

No. And for two reasons.

The first: Genesis is not the foundation. The whole weight of this thing does not sit on the first chapter, it sits on the exodus, on the event that was seen, that was handed down, that a whole people built its life around remembering. If you prove to me tomorrow the mechanism of the first cell or the first instant of the universe, down to the last decimal, you have touched nothing, because I never hung anything there. I have no stake in whether the world is six thousand years old or fourteen billion, whether it was made by a word or by a bang and four billion years of evolution. Maybe this way, maybe that. What does it matter how the universe was made? No one was there to testify to it, and no one rests his faith on it. But the exodus had witnesses, and it is on that basis that we believe.

The second, and the deeper one: Genesis itself was never a mechanism either. It is not a lab report. It says the world was created, and by whom, and therefore that it is given, that it has an owner and it has meaning. That is not the same kind of statement as "here is the chain of causes that led from energy to matter to life." When a physicist fills in that chain to the last detail, he has not refuted Genesis any more than analyzing the ink of a letter refutes that someone wrote it. The day someone proves the mechanism of the beginning, I will say: beautiful, now I know how He did it.

And that is before we have even asked which reading of Genesis we are talking about, because there is not one, there are at least ten. Ask me the age of the world and I will answer honestly: I do not know, I was not there. But here are a few of the readings already laid down in the tradition, long before modern science asked the question. Rashi, on "a thousand years in Your eyes are like yesterday," says a day of the Holy One is a thousand of our years, so that "the six days" are not our days, and the sun itself only appears on the fourth. Ramban distinguishes between "the days of the world," the six days of creation, and "the years of generation upon generation," human history, two different kinds of time. The Kabbalah, in Sefer HaTemunah and in the Arizal, speaks of shemittot, cosmic cycles of thousands of years that preceded ours; and Rabbi Yitzchak of Akko, counting in divine years, arrives at a number astonishingly close to science's fifteen billion. The sages tell that for nine hundred and seventy-four generations before the world was created the Holy One was building worlds and destroying them, and some want to see in that the big bang or evolution. The Tiferet Yisrael reconciled the fossil record with the Torah. There is a religious physicist who argues that by relativity, because of shifts in the flow of time, our billions of years compress to exactly six days. And there are those who insist the six days were really six days like ours. And the sages in Chullin say that all the works of creation were created in their full stature, the world made mature, Adam like a twenty-year-old and the trees already grown, so that measuring its age by today's rate is already an error of method. And in truth, I assume that God, who is all-powerful, could have made the world in a single instant, with all the trees and thoughts and memories already inside it. Right? I don't know.

And you do not need to decide between them, because the text itself does not presume to decide. The Zohar says the chapter of creation is "the glory of God is to conceal a matter," and only from the creation of man is it "the glory of kings to search a matter out." There is almost no plain sense there; most of the verses are expounded in Kabbalah and speak of entirely other things. The interpretive tradition does not deal with physical questions, and the Torah does not presume to be a science book. The sages themselves consulted the science of their day, and never claimed the verses carried scientific authority. So whoever demands a precise cosmological accounting from Genesis is demanding something the text never offered and the tradition never claimed. And if in the end science and Judaism arrive at a big bang or at evolution, I have no problem accepting it. But again, I do not believe because of that. I believe because we came out of Egypt.

And notice who makes exactly the same mistake here, from both sides. The fundamentalist defending six literal days against geology, and the atheist who thinks that if he refuted six literal days he refuted God, both are reading Genesis as a competing physics book. Both took an assertion about ownership and meaning and turned it into a hypothesis about mechanism. And the moment they did that, they are already arguing about the wrong thing.

## You don't re-prove a diploma

And if I am going to ground my faith, I had better do it thoroughly.

And the question does come: fine, but why should anyone accept the testimony now, thousands of years downstream?

Because that is how established things work. A covenant gets founded once, on what one generation witnessed, and then it gets carried. You do not re-cut it every morning. After you finish a degree, you do not sit the exam again every time someone asks whether you can read. That is what the diploma is for.

This is why the tradition is built almost entirely out of memory of the founding event: there are thirty-one commandments to remember the exodus, among them the first of the Ten Commandments, Shabbat, tefillin, and the mitzvah of sukkah. The whole of Passover is devoted to remembering the exodus, and there is a special commandment on seder night to tell of it. The point is not nostalgia. The point is that the founding event is the evidence, and the remembering is how a founding event stays load-bearing across centuries.

And the evidence is not one book. It is a nation. A whole people of millions keeps dozens of commandments whose only purpose is to remember a single event, retells it on a fixed night every year, and builds its calendar around it. That is not a story someone wrote down once. It is a binding document, testimony, transmitted from generation to generation, the way a court takes testimony, except the witnesses number in the millions and the chain of custody runs three thousand years without a break. A father does not lie to his son about where the family came from, and the son does not lie to his.

And imagine what it would take to forge a tradition like that. You would have to convince a whole people that it came out of a foreign land by wondrous miracles, and received a Torah it had never heard of, the whole of which is to remember an event that never happened, God forbid. And if you convince only one man, or one family, the story does not hold. You have to convince a nation. And it is not just a story. It is a binding book of law for every matter, from the laws of marriage to the strict laws of kashrut.

And it is not the inheritance of the Jews alone. The exodus is a shared cornerstone of the three great religions. Christianity adopted the whole of the Hebrew Bible, the exodus inside it; the New Testament returns to it again and again, and Jesus himself sits down to a Passover seder. Islam tells the story of Musa and Pharaoh throughout the Quran, as one of its central narratives. So it is not a handful of believers who hold this event, but something like half of humanity, across three traditions, each transmitting it through its own channels.

Imagine what it would have taken to convince that many people to accept a new religion founded on an event they had never heard of. Something as large as a whole nation walking out of an empire would have had to leave a great mark on humanity, and surely no one would have accepted it unless he already knew of it.

If you have a more reliable way to carry a fact across that much time, name it, because the standard being demanded of this one is a standard nothing else in history meets. We accept exactly this kind of evidence everywhere else without blinking. The memory of the Holocaust rests on a thinner apparatus than the exodus, fewer centuries and fewer institutions, and no one seriously argues that a few generations from now its denial should count as reasonable doubt.

And it is not the unfalsifiable hand-waving its critics assume, either. The Torah places bets in history. Prophecy is a bet that can lose, a statement about what will happen in time, exposed to time. The Torah prophesies that Israel will be exiled and return, which is what happened. It also says that the end of the world as we know it will come in two hundred and thirty-four years. A faith that says "this will come to pass" has put something on the table that the world can knock over. That is the opposite of a claim engineered to be safe.

## So who, exactly, did science beat?

No one. Science, measured against its own reasoning, is one of the most magnificent things our species has done, and it stands on its own without needing a defeated rival to stand on. It simply never had a religious opponent in the ring, because religion was not offering a competing mechanism to be outflanked. "God of the gaps" strikes at a straw man the serious believer never held. The believer was not using God to plug holes in physics. He was standing inside a covenant, watching physics do its excellent work inside a world he reads as given.

## You argue with God, not for Him

Which brings us back to Tevye. He does not reason his way to God, and he does not defend Him like a theorem under attack. He argues with Him, constantly, on the road, in the barn, in the middle of his own ruin: a running complaint addressed to the second party of a contract he has no intention of leaving. That is what belief looks like from the inside. Not "I have a better explanation than yours." Something closer to "I was there, we made a deal, and I am still in it, and I have some things to say to You about how it is going."

Science never threatened that, because science was never speaking to it. The man who thinks he chose evidence over faith has usually just chosen a version of faith that does not share its roots with the Jewish religion.
