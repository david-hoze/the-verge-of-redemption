# And That Is How God Runs the World

*Rebbe Nachman's Answer to Kafka, by Way of Everything Everywhere All at Once*

*Everything Everywhere All at Once (2022). Directed by Daniel Kwan and Daniel Scheinert. Starring Michelle Yeoh, Ke Huy Quan, Stephanie Hsu, Jamie Lee Curtis. A laundromat owner drowning in receipts discovers every life she could have lived, and her daughter, who saw all of it first, has concluded that nothing matters and baked the conclusion into a black hole shaped like a bagel. The weapons that win the war are googly eyes, a raccoon, and deliberate acts of silliness - which turns out to be a precise two-hundred-year-old prescription.*

There is a moment in *Everything Everywhere All at Once* when the villain explains herself, and the explanation is airtight. Jobu Tupaki has seen every universe. She has been everything, everywhere - a movie star, a chef, a rock, a woman with hot dogs for fingers - and from the top of that mountain of total experience she reports back: it's noise. No version matters, because every version exists. So she put everything on a bagel, and the bagel collapsed into a black hole, and she would like to fall into it, please.

Nobody in the film out-argues her. That is worth noticing. Her mother does not defeat her with a better philosophy. The counterattack, when it comes, is a pair of googly eyes.

I want to show that this exact war - the person who has seen too much and concluded that nothing means anything, against the person armed with deliberate silliness - was fought two hundred years ago by Rebbe Nachman of Breslov, and that he left us both halves in writing: a story that answers the nihilist, and a prescription that explains the googly eyes.

## The man arrested for nothing

First, the case for the bagel, made by its greatest attorney.

"Someone must have been telling lies about Josef K., for without having done anything wrong he was arrested one fine morning." That is the opening of Kafka's *The Trial*, and it is the cleanest statement ever written of a specific kind of suffering. Not pain - pointlessness. Josef K. is never tortured. He is processed. The court has offices, clerks, procedures, hierarchies; every link in the bureaucratic chain is fully staffed and fully explained. The only thing missing is the charge. He dies in a quarry without ever learning what he did, and the meaning of his case recedes at exactly the speed he approaches it.

That is the Kafka world: perfect administration of a verdict no one can read. And Kafka's honest conclusion is that the receding is the whole story. There is no room at the top of the courthouse with your file in it. The corridor is all there is.

The two men are closer than they look. Martin Buber published Rebbe Nachman's tales in German in 1906, when Kafka was twenty-three; a century later Rodger Kamenetz wrote a whole book, *Burnt Books*, pairing the two of them. The title points at the strangest thing they share: each man wrote a book and ordered it destroyed. Rebbe Nachman's Hasidim, who held every word of his to be holy, obeyed - the Burned Book is ash, and we know only that it existed. Kafka's best friend, who loved him, refused, and published everything. The rebbe was obeyed and the novelist was loved, and you can spend a long time deciding which of them was better understood.

## The man evicted in midwinter

Now the story. Rebbe Nachman's Hasidim preserved it from a conversation about the hidden ways of God - about the fact that no mind can grasp them - and it goes like this.

A poor Jew, burdened with children, leased the village tavern from the local nobleman, lived in it, and fed his family from it. Every three years the lease expired and went up for public auction, and every three years nobody bid against him - not even the gentiles - because everyone knew the tavern was all that family had, and they pitied him. Notice the world of this story before it breaks: the auction is open, the market is real, and year after year, mercy quietly outbids money. Then one year another Jew raised the price and took the lease, and the poor man was thrown out in the middle of winter, with his small children.

Then comes the detail the Hasidim remembered as the wonder, the one that turns the story from sad to scandalous. The man who took the tavern had no children - for years he had none. And that year, the year he did this to a poor family, he was granted a son.

That is the whole story. No lightning strikes. No ruin arrives. There is no scene, years later, where the account balances. The wrongdoer is rewarded in the most intimate currency there is, the evicted family is still standing in the snow, and the story ends. And Rebbe Nachman ended it with one sentence, which the Hasidim kept in the Yiddish he said it in: *Un azoy firt Got di velt.* And that is how God runs the world.

Everything in that story belongs to Kafka. The arbitrary loss, the innocent family processed out of its life, the reward flowing uphill to the man who deserves it least, the explanation that never comes. Kafka could have published it without changing a word. He would only have needed to change the tone of the final sentence - because Rebbe Nachman did not say it bitterly. He told the story as an illustration of the wonders of God's hidden ways, and then refused, completely, to shrink the wonder into a lesson. He did not say the boy would grow up to repay the family. He did not promise the poor man his reward in the next world. He did not explain at all. He said: and that is how God runs the world.

Hold that refusal up to the light, because the whole answer to the bagel is inside it. Rebbe Nachman grants Kafka everything - the entire file. What he disputes is one word: *meaningless*. And he disputes it without producing the meaning. That is the originality of the move. A theodicy would have argued with Kafka: wait long enough and the books balance. Rebbe Nachman does not argue, because on Kafka's chosen field - the field of visible evidence - Kafka wins. He says instead that the meaning is not late; it is sealed. It sits above the ceiling of the intellect, his included, and a mind standing under a ceiling has exactly two honest options: to say "I cannot see past it," or to claim that the ceiling is the sky. Kafka stands in front of the locked door and testifies that there is nothing behind it. Rebbe Nachman locks the same door and says: the wonder is inside, and you are not getting it. The lock is the same lock. The inventory is not.

And notice which story he chose to tell. A man speaking about hidden providence gets to pick his example, and he passed over every story where the loss turns out to be the rescue - the whole genre where the hidden hand shows itself in the last scene - and picked the one where the hand stays hidden and the only visible movement runs the wrong way. Because a rescue story teaches that God's ways are temporarily invisible, and that is not what he said. He said they are above the intellect. Not late. Above.

Now Jobu Tupaki can be diagnosed precisely. She has read every file in every universe, and from the totality of her reading she concludes there is nothing to read. In Rebbe Nachman's terms, her mistake is not in the reading; it is that she took the ceiling of her intellect - and a multiversal intellect is still an intellect - for the ceiling of the world. The bagel is an audit certifying absence. The auditor has never been outside the building.

## Why jokes?

Which leaves the googly eyes. If the meaning is real but sealed above the ceiling, how are you supposed to live under it? Here is the second teaching, and it is not a mood - it is a ruling. Likutey Moharan Tinyana 24: *It is a great mitzvah to be in joy always.* Every illness that comes upon a person, Rebbe Nachman says there, comes from the breakdown of joy. And because human nature is dragged toward black bile by the injuries of time - his words - a person must force himself toward joy with all his strength, and make himself happy by any means he can find, *even with silly things*. Milei d'shtuta. And the teaching ends at the end of history: in the time to come, God Himself will stand at the head of the circle-dance of the righteous. The dance is not a metaphor for the redemption. It is the position God takes in it.

That is why telling a silly joke is a divine action and not a coping mechanism. Follow the logic of the two teachings together. Seriousness is the posture of a person whose accounting of the world is complete - and if your accounting is complete and it reads *ruined, for nothing*, then despair is not a weakness, it is arithmetic. Jobu Tupaki is perfectly serious. The bagel is a balanced ledger. But Rebbe Nachman has just told you a story your ledger cannot survive: one village, one winter, one lease, and the books already refuse to close. If your accounting cannot read a tavern auction, its verdict on the whole of your life is not a verdict worth despairing over. The silly act is the act that declines to certify the false total. It is not a denial of the incomprehensible world. It is the only response that matches it.

## The improbability engine

Now watch the movie again with both teachings in hand, because the Daniels did not film a mood either. They filmed a mechanic.

In *Everything Everywhere All at Once*, you jump to another version of yourself by doing the least probable thing you can do. Eat the chapstick. Put your shoes on the wrong feet. Give yourself four paper cuts. Profess your love to the IRS auditor. The film's physics could not be more explicit: the life you are stuck in is a groove, probability is the wall of the groove, and the only thing that jumps the wall is an act too absurd for the groove to predict. Every power Evelyn gains, she gains by doing something a dignified adult would never do.

Rebbe Nachman's prescription, filmed. Black bile is also a groove - anyone who has been inside it knows that its walls are made of plausibility, that every despairing thought is *reasonable*, each one following from the last. You cannot argue your way out, because argument is the groove's home turf. You have to do the thing the despair cannot predict: make yourself a fool, sing something, tell the stupid joke. The silliness is not the reward of joy. It is the vehicle - the jump between the universe where you are finished and the universe where you are alive. He wrote the prescription two hundred years ago; the Daniels filmed it with a budget and won seven Oscars for it.

The Daniels even chose the right venue. A laundromat under audit is a tavern under auction - one small leased business with a whole family inside it, and everything that can be lost, mid-winter, all at once. And the film knows the difference between its two ways of seeing what happens there. Jobu saw every universe and certified the absence. Evelyn sees the same everything, and in the film's gentlest scene she answers it from the bottom: two rocks on a lifeless cliff, no music, no dialogue, the whole absurd cosmos laid out in silence - and then one rock slides a googly eye at the other. That is Rebbe Nachman's last sentence, performed with craft-store supplies. The facts on that cliff are all Jobu's facts: nothing lives here, nothing speaks here, nobody is coming. The googly eye does not dispute a single one of them. It declines to certify the total - and the silence turns into a joke between a mother and a daughter.

## Standing under the ceiling

We do not get the reveal. That is the honest place to end, because the innkeeper never got one either - no later scene was added, no meaning was ever found, and the whole point of both teachings is that it will not unseal on our schedule. Each of us carries a version of that winter: the loss with no line in the accounting, the years that look wasted, the wrong that was answered, as far as anyone can see, with a reward. Kafka is right about how it reads from inside. Rebbe Nachman asks us to believe one thing more: that it reads that way because we are under the ceiling, not because there is nothing above it. We cannot verify that tonight. We can only choose our posture under the sealed sky - and the tradition's ruling on the correct posture is on the books: something silly. A joke, a song, googly eyes. Said in despair, "that is how God runs the world" is the bitterest sentence a person can speak. Said in wonder, it is the only one that survives the winter. Same words. Different eyes.
