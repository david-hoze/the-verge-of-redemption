# The Snake That Eats Its Own Tail

*Rabbi Akiva's Answer to Fatalism, by Way of Predestination*

*Predestination (2014). Directed by Michael and Peter Spierig. Starring Ethan Hawke, Sarah Snook, Noah Taylor. A temporal agent's final assignment closes the tightest loop in science fiction: a recruit who turns out to be his own mother, father, daughter, and pursuer. Based on Robert A. Heinlein's "'-All You Zombies-'". A film that grants determinism everything it ever asked for, and then shows a person choosing inside it.*

There is a scene in *Predestination* where a man is handed the offer every wounded person has rehearsed in the dark. The Bartender leans across the bar and asks: what if I could put him in front of you - the man who ruined your life? If I guaranteed you'd get away with it, would you kill him? John says yes, of course. His ledger justifies it. He was abandoned at an orphanage as an infant. The one person who ever loved him - back when he was Jane - vanished without a word, leaving her pregnant. The birth nearly killed her; the surgeons discovered she was intersex, and complications forced the reassignment that took the life she knew. Then someone stole her newborn daughter from the nursery. One man is at the root of it. Yes, he would kill him.

So the Bartender takes him back to 1963 and John waits for the seducer to show up. And the man he has come to kill is himself.

Then the film does the thing it will be remembered for. John stands in front of Jane knowing everything - that he will love her, that he will vanish, that the vanishing is the engine of every grief he came here to avenge - and he stays. He does not fall in love with her; falling is what you do when you don't know. He knows. He chooses her, wound and all, on purpose.

Philosophers have a name for the trap the film has sprung. It is the oldest argument against free will there is: if the future is already fixed - already known - then choice is an illusion, because you cannot choose against a fact. *Predestination* grants that premise more completely than any film ever made. The future is not merely known; it has already happened, the characters read their own files, and the loop is physically unbreakable. And inside that sealed loop the film locates a choice, an origin, and a strange consolation. What I want to show here is that all three of the film's teachings - that a foreseen life can still be chosen, that something can come from nothing, and that the worst things that happen to you are the manufacture of you - were asserted nineteen centuries ago by one man, Rabbi Akiva, who bit every bullet the fatalists ever loaded.

## The trade

The problem is older than theology. Aristotle already saw that if "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is true tonight, then tomorrow is not open; the battle is as settled as yesterday. Add an all-knowing God and the argument sharpens into what philosophers call theological fatalism. Boethius wrestled with it in a prison cell in 524 while awaiting execution; Nelson Pike formalized it in 1965 into the version still taught in every philosophy of religion course. If God knew a thousand years ago what you will do tomorrow, then doing otherwise would require you to change what God knew a thousand years ago. Nobody has power over the past. Therefore nobody has power over tomorrow.

The history of responses is a history of trades. Boethius moved God outside of time: an eternal present is not *fore*knowledge, so the past is never held hostage - sell the "fore," keep the freedom. Ockham reclassified God's past knowledge as a "soft fact" that our choices somehow still reach - keep both, sell the ordinary meaning of "past." The open theists, the honest moderns, simply sell the knowledge: God knows everything knowable, and future free choices are not knowable. And the Calvinists sell the other clause: God's knowledge stands, and the will is not free - grace moves it, and what feels like choosing is the sensation of being moved.

Notice what every one of these positions has in common. Each buys its coherence by selling one of the two claims, at a discount, quietly. Through the whole twenty-three-century negotiation, one option stayed off the table as simply unthinkable: pay full price for both. Assert complete foreknowledge and complete freedom, at full strength, with no mechanism connecting them - and stand there.

## The vav

Rabbi Akiva, Pirkei Avot 3:15: *hakol tzafui, veharshut netunah*. All is foreseen, and freedom is granted.

Four words and a vav. No argument, no mechanism, no discount. The entire foreknowledge debate is a war over that single letter - every philosopher in the previous section is trying to weaken one side of it so the sentence can be parsed. Rabbi Akiva declines to weaken either side, which means he is asserting a sentence he cannot parse. He knows it. He does it anyway.

The Rambam, codifying this in Hilchot Teshuvah, explains why the join cannot be exhibited even in principle: God's knowledge is not a faculty God has, the way our knowledge is something we have. God's knowledge is God's essence - and we can no more grasp how His knowing works than we can grasp what He is. Both facts are certain; the seam between them is not in our jurisdiction. The Ra'avad, in his gloss, is unimpressed - not with the doctrine but with the decision to print it: the author began a matter he did not know how to finish; better to have left the innocent in their innocence. It is the medieval version of a note every editor has written in a margin: you raised the question, and now the reader is stuck with it too.

It took Rebbe Nachman of Breslov, six centuries later, to say what the Rambam's move actually implies - and it is stranger and better than a shrug. In Likutey Moharan 21 he divides knowledge into what can enter the mind and what is too large to enter: the *makifin*, the surrounding intellects, which circle the mind the way an atmosphere circles a planet. The reconciliation of foreknowledge and choice is a makif - not a hard question but a structurally external one, knowledge that does not fit inside a created head. And then the reversal: this, he says, is itself the essence of choice. Freedom is possible *because* the reconciliation cannot enter. The gap in your understanding is not where the doctrine embarrasses itself; it is the clearing where your will stands. In the world to come, he says, the surrounding knowledge finally enters - the righteous sit with their crowns on their heads - and on that day there is no more choosing. Answer the question and you abolish the chooser. The vav is not holding two clauses together. It is holding a space open.

## The man in front of you

Now go back to 1963 and watch the scene again with that in hand.

The film has removed every veil that normally hides the fatalist's problem. John is not a man with a vague premonition; he has lived the other side of this encounter. He knows the love, the vanishing, the birth, the theft, in the first person. Determinism usually has to be argued for; here it is simply the case, on screen, notarized. If foreknowledge canceled choice, this scene would be the cancellation - a puppet watching its own strings.

That is not what the scene is. Knowing changes the act's category. A man who falls for a woman and later abandons her has done something that happened to him as much as to her. John has no such shelter. He stands where the Bartender put him - in front of the man who ruined Jane's life - and instead of pulling a trigger he walks over and ruins it, and redeems it, himself, with full knowledge. It is the least deniable act in the film. The foreknowledge didn't shrink his agency. It concentrated it: everything that a person usually discovers too late, he carries in advance, so the only thing left in the scene is the choosing.

Rabbi Akiva died the same way. The Talmud in Berakhot relates that when the Romans combed his flesh with iron combs, he recited the Shema, and his students - watching, horrified - asked: our teacher, even here? He answered: all my days I was troubled by this verse, "with all your soul" - even if He takes your soul. I said: when will it come to my hand, that I may fulfill it? *Now that it has come to my hand, shall I not fulfill it?*

Read that answer slowly. He is not saying he accepts his death. He is saying he has known this ending his whole life - carried it, rehearsed it, waited for it - and now that the foreseen moment has arrived, he claims it as an act. The foreknowledge is precisely what converts his death from something that happens to him into something he does. That is John outside the college in 1963. The scene the film stages as a paradox - he knew, and still he chose - is not a paradox in Rabbi Akiva's grammar. It is the maximum case of choice. What you know is coming is the one thing you can do entirely on purpose.

## The snake with no head

The Bartender names the film's structure himself: the snake that eats its own tail, forever and ever. Trace Jane's origin and you find the film's second teaching. She was left at the orphanage as a baby - by the Agent, who took her from the nursery. The baby is Jane's own daughter. Her father is John, who is Jane. The jukebox in the bar plays "I'm My Own Grandpa," and for once in the history of needle-drops the song is underselling the situation.

Here is what should actually keep you up at night about that loop. Inside it, nothing is unexplained. Every event has a complete, sufficient cause: the baby exists because Jane bore her, Jane exists because the baby was delivered to 1945, John abandons Jane because John abandoned Jane. Every link of the chain is accounted for. And the chain as a whole came from nowhere. There is no first loop, no version one, no moment the snake was assembled. The film never shows an origin because there is no origin to show. Perfect internal explanation; zero external explanation. Something came from nothing, and the something is a person.

This is not a fantasy problem; it is our problem. Every cosmology on offer has the loop's structure. An eternal universe explains each state by a prior state, forever, and never explains why the series exists. A cyclic universe is Jane with better production values. Even a universe with a first moment only relocates the question to the first moment. Explaining every link has nothing to do with explaining the chain - the film just makes the gap impossible to hide, because it bends the chain into a circle and lets you check every link yourself, twice.

The tradition's answer to this is routinely misread. Creation from nothing - *yesh me'ayin* - sounds like a claim about ignition: a first cause lights the fuse, and the machine runs itself from there. That is not the claim. The liturgy says God "renews, in His goodness, every day, continuously, the work of creation" - present tense, standing appointment. Creation is not the first link in the chain; it is what the whole chain hangs from, now, at every point equally. A beginningless universe is no objection to it, because the doctrine never needed a beginning - a loop hangs from nothing-visible just as completely as a line does. And the Nothing has a name. In the Kabbalistic vocabulary, *ayin* is not absence; it is what the divine looks like from the side of the creature - the un-thing out of which the something continuously arrives. The film builds a person suspended from no cause and calls it a paradox. The tradition looks at the same picture and calls it Tuesday: that is what everything is, all the time. The only unusual thing about Jane is that in her case you can see it.

## The fox in the ruins

Which leaves the film's cruelest arithmetic, and its third teaching.

Count Jane's catastrophes again: orphaned, seduced, abandoned, cut open, remade, robbed of her child. Now run the counterfactuals, which the film has rigged so that you can. If the lover stays, there is no bitter writer in the bar in 1970, no recruit, no trip to 1963, no daughter. If the daughter is not stolen, she is never delivered to 1945, and Jane never exists to lose her. Repair any one grief in the ledger and Jane is not happier - she is *nothing*. Her tragedies are not obstacles her life had to survive. They are the manufacturing process of her existence. The wound and the person are the same object, seen from different points on the loop.

Rabbi Akiva owns this teaching too. The Talmud in Berakhot has him traveling with a donkey, a rooster, and a lamp. A town refuses him lodging; he says, all that the Merciful One does, He does for the best, and sleeps in a field. Wind kills the lamp, a cat kills the rooster, a lion kills the donkey - for the best, he says each time, absurdly. That night raiders sack the town. The lamp, the rooster, the donkey - any one of them would have given him away. Every loss, it turns out, was load-bearing. He could not see how, and said the sentence anyway; the seeing came later, from the far side of the night.

And once, the far side was shown to him early. Four sages, the Talmud says in Makkot, walked on the Temple Mount after the destruction and saw a fox come out of the Holy of Holies. Three wept. Akiva laughed. He explained: the prophecy of consolation is formally chained to the prophecy of ruin - until Uriah's "Zion shall be plowed as a field" was fulfilled, I feared Zechariah's old men and children in the streets of Jerusalem would never come. Now that the ruin has arrived, the consolation is guaranteed. His colleagues saw an ending. He read the same fox from the other side of the loop, where the ruin is not the story's collapse but its load-bearing wall. Akiva, they told him, you have comforted us; Akiva, you have comforted us.

Be careful with this teaching, because it is unbearable in the wrong mouth. Said from a safe distance about someone else's wounds, "it was all for the best" is not wisdom, it is anesthesia for the speaker. What earns the sentence, in the Talmud and in the film, is that the one who says it is the one who bleeds. Akiva's biography is the ledger he was reading from: a descendant of converts, an illiterate shepherd until forty, a man who buried twenty-four thousand students and died under Roman combs - and the entire Oral Torah, the Talmud says, runs through him. Remove one humiliation from his file and there is no Rabbi Akiva. And Jane says it with her hands. She is not told, gently, by someone unbroken, that her losses had a purpose. She is the one who, standing in 1963 with the whole file memorized, administers the losses to herself - because on the far side of them is the person she cannot exist without becoming. Nobody grants her tragedies meaning. She signs for them.

## Now that it has come to my hand

I do not read my file over a bar in 1970. Neither do you. That is the disanalogy, and it is smaller than it looks. Because everyone I know has stood at least once where John stands outside the college: in front of a path they could not justify, could not parse, could not defend to a committee - and knew, with a certainty that arrived from nowhere the way Jane did, that it was theirs to walk. The tradition's claim is that this is not a bug in our decision-making. It is what being inside the loop feels like from a link's point of view: foreseen from a place you cannot see, free at the only point you occupy, suspended - all of it, the whole chain - from Nothing.

The fatalists keep asking how both clauses of Rabbi Akiva's sentence can be true, and the honest answer is that no one knows, and Rebbe Nachman's sharper answer is that the day we know is the day the second clause expires. Until then the vav holds the space open, and in that space a man can stand in front of his own foreseen life - the love, the loss, the whole notarized file - the way Akiva stood in front of the one verse that had troubled him all his days, and ask the only question freedom has ever consisted of: now that it has come to my hand, shall I not fulfill it?
