# The Laughter That Shakes the Forest

*Rebbe Nachman's Answer to Kafka, by Way of Everything Everywhere All at Once*

*Everything Everywhere All at Once (2022). Directed by Daniel Kwan and Daniel Scheinert. Starring Michelle Yeoh, Ke Huy Quan, Stephanie Hsu, Jamie Lee Curtis. A laundromat owner drowning in receipts discovers every life she could have lived, and her daughter, who saw all of it first, has concluded that nothing matters and baked the conclusion into a black hole shaped like a bagel. The weapons that win the war are googly eyes, a raccoon, and deliberate acts of silliness - which turns out to be a precise two-hundred-year-old prescription.*

There is a moment in *Everything Everywhere All at Once* when the villain explains herself, and the explanation is airtight. Jobu Tupaki has seen every universe. She has been everything, everywhere - a movie star, a chef, a rock, a woman with hot dogs for fingers - and from the top of that mountain of total experience she reports back: it's noise. No version matters, because every version exists. So she put everything on a bagel, and the bagel collapsed into a black hole, and she would like to fall into it, please.

Nobody in the film out-argues her. That is worth noticing. Her mother does not defeat her with a better philosophy. The counterattack, when it comes, is a pair of googly eyes.

I want to show that this exact war - the person who has seen too much and concluded that nothing means anything, against the person armed with deliberate silliness - was fought two hundred years ago by Rebbe Nachman of Breslov, and that he left us both halves in writing: a story that answers the nihilist, and a prescription that explains the googly eyes.

## The man arrested for nothing

First, the case for the bagel, made by its greatest attorney.

"Someone must have been telling lies about Josef K., for without having done anything wrong he was arrested one fine morning." That is the opening of Kafka's *The Trial*, and it is the cleanest statement ever written of a specific kind of suffering. Not pain - pointlessness. Josef K. is never tortured. He is processed. The court has offices, clerks, procedures, hierarchies; every link in the bureaucratic chain is fully staffed and fully explained. The only thing missing is the charge. He dies in a quarry without ever learning what he did, and the meaning of his case recedes at exactly the speed he approaches it.

That is the Kafka world: perfect administration of a verdict no one can read. And Kafka's honest conclusion is that the receding is the whole story. There is no room at the top of the courthouse with your file in it. The corridor is all there is.

The two men are closer than they look. Martin Buber published Rebbe Nachman's tales in German in 1906, when Kafka was twenty-three; a century later Rodger Kamenetz wrote a whole book, *Burnt Books*, pairing the two of them. The title points at the strangest thing they share: each man wrote a book and ordered it destroyed. Rebbe Nachman's Hasidim, who held every word of his to be holy, obeyed - the Burned Book is ash, and we know only that it existed. Kafka's best friend, who loved him, refused, and published everything. The rebbe was obeyed and the novelist was loved, and you can spend a long time deciding which of them was better understood.

## The prince thrown out for nothing

Now the story. Rebbe Nachman told a tale about a man who loses everything, and he built it on a foundation of pure Kafka: a midwife swaps two newborns - the king's son and the servant's son - for no reason at all. Not for money, not for revenge. The text says she did it "to see what would come of it." The whole catastrophe begins as a whim.

The true prince grows up a servant. The servant's son grows up and takes the throne, hears the rumor of the exchange, and hounds the innocent man out of the country. And the exiled man, sitting with his losses, runs the accounting that any honest reader of Kafka will recognize: *If I am the king's son, this should not have happened to me. And if I am not the king's son, then what is my sin? Why should I be a fugitive for nothing?*

Read that complaint carefully, because Rebbe Nachman has hidden a diagnosis in it. The man is not destroyed by the exile. He is destroyed by the *for nothing*. The text says explicitly that he took himself to drink and to brothels because he was banished without cause - because the ledger of his life stopped adding up. Nihilism, in this story, is not a philosophy someone talks you into. It is what the heart does with an event that has no line in the accounting. Jobu Tupaki is not a supervillain; she is this prince with more universes.

Then the story does something Kafka never permits. The losses keep coming - the money runs out, a dream orders him to sell his fine clothes and hire himself out at a fair, he ends up driving another man's cattle on foot - and the losses begin to *steer*. Two animals bolt into a forest. He chases them deeper and deeper, terrified, sleeping in trees, until a man of the forest meets him and says: stop chasing. Those are not cattle at all. Those are your sins, leading you. You have already received what was yours - now come with me, and you will come to what is fit for you.

Every night in that forest, the animals roar, and the roaring is terror itself. Until the two exiles listen closely - and the text slows down to describe the act of inclining an ear - and the roar resolves into a melody, a song of such sweetness that every pleasure in the world is nothing beside it. The forest did not change. The ears did. And every night, just before dawn, a laughter rolls through the trees so enormous that the trunks shake. The forest man explains: it is the day laughing at the night. The night asks the day, why is it that when you come, I have no name? And the day laughs - and then it is morning.

The night's question never gets an answer. It gets a laugh, and then it gets morning. Hold that; we will need it.

The ending pays off every loss. The wandering has delivered the prince to a kingdom whose wise old king left a riddle instead of an heir: a garden that chases out everyone who enters, and a throne room no one understands. The prince, who traded away his one treasure for the ability to understand one thing from another, looks at the throne room and sees what no one else saw: nothing is broken. Nothing is missing. The rose is at the bottom of the throne when it belongs at the top; the bed, the table, the lamp, each needs to move just a little. It is a laundromat's worth of trouble: nothing ruined, everything in the wrong pile. He orders each thing moved to its place - and the whole room bursts into the melody from the forest, and they crown him. His first words as king are: *Now I understand that I am truly the king's son.*

Now count what the exile cost him, the way we counted Jane's losses in *Predestination*. If he is not banished for nothing, he never sinks; if he never sinks, he never chases the cattle; if he never chases the cattle, he never hears the melody, never gains the understanding, never reaches the kingdom that only that understanding could win. The *for nothing* was the road. There was never a wasted step on it - there was only a man who could not yet read his own itinerary.

This is Rebbe Nachman's answer to the bagel, and notice how much he grants to get there. He grants the arbitrary catastrophe, the innocent victim, the missing charge, the years of wandering - the entire Kafka file. He disputes one word: *meaningless*. The events were never meaningless; they were meaningful in a script the man could not read while he was inside it. Kafka says the corridor is all there is. Rebbe Nachman says the corridor is the route.

And then he does the most Kafka thing imaginable: he refuses to explain his own story. After telling it, he said that the tale is a great wonder, that it is all one, that the matters are deep - "and there is more, and there is no need to reveal it to you." A story about hidden meaning that hides its own meaning. Both writers leave you standing at a locked door. But Kafka's door, as far as he will testify, has nothing behind it, and Rebbe Nachman signs off like a man locking a treasury: there is more, and you are not getting it tonight. The lock is the same. The inventory is not.

## Why jokes?

Which leaves the googly eyes. If the meaning is real but sealed, how are you supposed to live in front of the sealed door? Here is the second teaching, and it is not a mood - it is a ruling. Likutey Moharan Tinyana 24: *It is a great mitzvah to be in joy always.* Every illness that comes upon a person, Rebbe Nachman says there, comes from the breakdown of joy. And because human nature is dragged toward black bile by the injuries of time - his words - a person must force himself toward joy with all his strength, and make himself happy by any means he can find, *even with silly things*. Milei d'shtuta. And the teaching ends at the end of history: in the time to come, God Himself will stand at the head of the circle-dance of the righteous. The dance is not a metaphor for the redemption. It is the position God takes in it.

That is why telling a silly joke is a divine action and not a coping mechanism. Follow the logic of the two teachings together. Seriousness is the posture of a person whose accounting of the world is complete - and if your accounting is complete and it reads *ruined, for nothing*, then despair is not a weakness, it is arithmetic. Jobu Tupaki is perfectly serious. The bagel is a balanced ledger. But if the meaning of your life is structurally hidden from you - if you are the prince mid-journey, holding an itinerary you cannot read - then your solemn accounting is wrong on its face, and the only honest posture is the one that refuses to certify it. The silly act is not a denial of the incomprehensible world. It is the only response that matches it. This is what the forest was teaching every morning: the night asks its unanswerable question, and the day does not answer it, because the answer does not fit inside the night. The day laughs - and then it is morning.

## The improbability engine

Now watch the movie again with both teachings in hand, because the Daniels did not film a mood either. They filmed a mechanic.

In *Everything Everywhere All at Once*, you jump to another version of yourself by doing the least probable thing you can do. Eat the chapstick. Put your shoes on the wrong feet. Give yourself four paper cuts. Profess your love to the IRS auditor. The film's physics could not be more explicit: the life you are stuck in is a groove, probability is the wall of the groove, and the only thing that jumps the wall is an act too absurd for the groove to predict. Every power Evelyn gains, she gains by doing something a dignified adult would never do.

Rebbe Nachman's prescription, filmed. Black bile is also a groove - anyone who has been inside it knows that its walls are made of plausibility, that every despairing thought is *reasonable*, each one following from the last. You cannot argue your way out, because argument is the groove's home turf. You have to do the thing the despair cannot predict: make yourself a fool, sing something, tell the stupid joke. The silliness is not the reward of joy. It is the vehicle - the jump between the universe where you are finished and the universe where you are alive. He ordered Jews to verse-jump in 1808; the Daniels put it on screen with a budget and won seven Oscars for it.

And the film knows the difference between its two ways of seeing everything, which is exactly the tale's difference. Jobu saw every universe and heard noise - she is the man in the forest who never inclined his ear, for whom the roar stayed roar, and the bagel is that undifferentiated everything pressed into a ring. Evelyn sees the same everything and, in the film's gentlest scene, answers it from the bottom: two rocks on a lifeless cliff, no music, no dialogue, the whole absurd cosmos laid out in silence - and then one rock slides a googly eye at the other. It is the day laughing at the night, performed with craft-store supplies. Same forest. Same roar. Different ears - and the noise resolves into the melody it was the whole time.

## Standing in front of the door

We do not get the reveal. That is the honest place to end, because the prince got his throne room and we are still mid-forest, and the whole point of both teachings is that the meaning does not unseal on our schedule. Each of us carries a file of events with no line in the accounting - the loss that led nowhere, the years that look wasted, the thing that happened for nothing. Kafka is right about how that file reads from inside. Rebbe Nachman asks us to believe one thing more: that it reads that way because we are inside it, that the cattle we are chasing are leading us somewhere, and that nothing in the room is broken - it is only, for now, in the wrong place. We cannot verify that tonight. We can only choose our posture in front of the sealed door. And the tradition's ruling on the correct posture is on the books: something silly. A joke, a song, googly eyes. The night asks why it has no name, and the day laughs, and then it is morning.
