## Focus

## What She's Actually Doing

Three articles in nine months. Each one does real work:

**"Morocco in the Afterlife of Anti-Colonial Romance"** (Mar 2026) - The strongest piece. Argues that postcolonial discourse contains a "durable hostility to functional sovereignty" - states that build working institutions without theatrical self-deprecation get accused of moral compromise. Traces how Moroccans organically started saying "Long live Israel" after being punished for competence at AFCON. Reads this as strategic realism, not servility. Pan-Africanism and pan-Arabism substituted romance for statecraft. Real political analysis, not identity performance.

**"Where Do We Begin Again?"** (Jul 2025) - Origin story. Abraham Accords true believer who watched peace partners celebrate October 7th. Bold section on Fairuz's Jerusalem song as narrative warfare rather than art. Weakest piece - leans on biography more than argument, and treats a song as a proposition rather than engaging with what it does emotionally.

**"President Trump May Have Just Outsmarted The Muslim Brotherhood"** (Oct 2025) - US reasserting itself as architect of peace rather than mediator.

## Assessment

The writing is real. She's not doing influencer peace-content. The Morocco piece earns her a read - the critique of ideological capture, romance substituting for statecraft, the hostility-to-functional-sovereignty thesis. She has a genuine intellectual project, not just a biography.

**Where she overlaps with the framework:**
- Postcolonial ideology's hostility to functional sovereignty maps onto the convergence trap
- Romance substituting for statecraft maps onto the substitution chain
- Her lived experience of the 2003 Casablanca bombings - radicalization arriving through gradual moral recoding, not spectacular violence - maps onto the thinning-of-the-walls pattern

**Where she's missing something:**
She's normalization-first, top-down. Abraham Accords as mechanism. She has a theory of *why the current peace opponents are wrong* (postcolonial capture, narrative warfare, conditional solidarity). She does NOT have a theory of *why peace obligates*. That's a different question entirely and it's the load-bearing one. Coordination without meaning underneath it is the exact failure mode described in "Survival Gets People in the Room."

## Meat to Give Her

Five articles that fill the gap between "opponents of peace are wrong" and "peace obligates":

1. **"Survival Gets People in the Room"** - Directly addresses her structural gap. The Abraham Accords are coordination. Coordination alone doesn't produce peace - meaning has to precede the mechanism. Every resistance movement she'd admire (French Resistance, civil rights) organized around meaning that was already there, not around the coordination itself. Morocco's competence works because there's something underneath it. What is it?

2. **"The Law That Walked"** - Her Morocco piece is about a state building functional sovereignty while pan-movements dissolve. This article shows how Jewish law survived the collapse of every institution that held it - through distributed governance, personal vouching, and a legal system that walked without a building. The mechanism she's looking for.

3. **"Philosophy Is a Commentary That Forgot"** - The postmodernism section maps directly onto her critique of postcolonial ideology. Derrida, Foucault, Baudrillard - systematic deconstruction of every stable framework for meaning with nothing offered to replace it. She's fighting the downstream effects. This is the upstream diagnosis.

4. **"Western Liberalism's Blind Spots Are Becoming AI's Design Flaws"** - She's arguing for the Abraham Accords, but the framework underneath is liberal internationalism. Value neutrality cannot guide governance. If the normalization framework doesn't have a shared theory of human flourishing underneath it, it's vulnerable to the same collapse she's documenting in pan-Africanism. The article shows this empirically.

5. **"The God Who Argues Back"** - If she's going to engage with fundamentalism on both sides (Shia millenarianism, Christian Zionist eschatology, Islamist radicalization), she needs the tool that distinguishes between tradition and fundamentalism. Fundamentalism conflates things the tradition kept separate. The tradition argues back.
